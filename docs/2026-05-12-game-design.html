2D Top-Down Fantasy RPG Simulator

Idle Fantasy Game Design Notes

Idle Fantasy is a quasi-idle RPG simulator where the player manages settlements that attract adventurers. Adventurers autonomously leave town, explore the world, fight monsters, collect loot, return to resupply, level up, and repeat.

The core fantasy is watching a living RPG world run on its own, then nudging the economy, roster, infrastructure, and risk profile so the adventurer ecosystem grows from a small village into a city-scale fantasy hub.

Player Fantasy Abstract observer, planner, and occasional spellcaster.
Core Tension Solo runs give higher rewards. Parties reduce risk but split gains.
Progression Village to town to city, with stronger services and recruits.
First Slice One town, one adventurer, one monster, one full loop.

Core Loop

Autonomous RPG Cycle
  1. Adventurers are hired manually from a settlement, spending player gold.
  2. Adventurers pick a goal based on level, health, gear, nearby threats, and available locations.
  3. They travel into the world.
  4. They fight monsters, explore ruins, or enter dungeons.
  5. They collect loot, gold, experience, and injuries.
  6. They decide whether to continue, retreat, or die based on risk and supplies.
  7. Survivors return to settlement.
  8. They buy potions, upgrade equipment, rest, level up, or retire.
  9. Settlement services improve, attracting stronger adventurers.

Player Role

Planner First

The player influences the simulation through long-term management decisions. The player is abstract, not a visible character in the world. Direct control should normally be limited, though spells can create short and intentional windows for intervention.

  • Hire adventurers manually with gold.
  • Upgrade village, town, and city services.
  • Fund shops, inns, clerics, training halls, guilds, and defenses.
  • Set broad policies such as risk tolerance, hunting zones, bounty priorities, and expedition funding.
  • Cast spells that temporarily affect the world, adventurers, monsters, or economy.
  • Watch individual adventurer stories emerge from autonomous decisions.

No Pause For Now

The simulation should keep running, which makes timing, risk, and intervention costs matter.

No-Money Fallback

If the player runs out of money, a downtrodden adventurer may be hireable: weak, desperate, cheap or free, and risky to rely on.

Later progression may let the abstract player build additional buildings, research upgrades, unlock automation, or auto-hire adventurers. Early gameplay should stay relatively idle and management-light.

Time Model

Hybrid

Idle Fantasy uses a hybrid time model: mechanics resolve on ticks, while movement and world time advance continuously so actors feel fluid and dynamic.

Tick-Based Mechanics

  • Attacks.
  • Casting.
  • Buffs and debuffs.
  • Cooldowns.
  • Other discrete gameplay resolutions.

Continuous Feel

  • Smooth travel.
  • Fluid animation.
  • Dynamic world movement.
  • Readable action between mechanical ticks.

Player Spells

Active Intervention

Player spells are active interventions layered on top of the idle simulation. They let the player influence outcomes without replacing the autonomous game loop.

Guidance

Reveal threats, highlight valuable ruins, improve target selection, or redirect an adventurer.

Protection

Shield an adventurer, reduce incoming damage, prevent ambushes, or improve escape chance.

World Influence

Slow monsters, bless a road, reveal hidden loot, or weaken a dungeon room.

Economy Influence

Boost shop stock, reduce upgrade cost, improve loot sale prices, or attract recruits.

Possession

Temporarily take direct control of an adventurer while the simulation continues around them.

Costs

Spells should use mana, favor, resources, rare relics, cooldown time, or opportunity cost.

Possession Spell

Temporary Control

During Possession

  • The selected adventurer stops autonomous decision-making.
  • The player can click to move.
  • The player can click to target enemies or objects.
  • The player can use that adventurer's available skills.
  • The adventurer still uses their own stats, gear, health, inventory, and risk.
  • The simulation continues around them.

When It Ends

  • Control returns to the adventurer AI.
  • The adventurer evaluates their current situation and resumes an appropriate state.
  • If hurt, overloaded, lost, or in danger, they may retreat rather than continue the player's plan.

Design Intent

Possession gives the player dramatic agency at key moments, stays time-limited so idle simulation remains the core identity, creates a reason to care about individual builds, and can be gated by progression, cooldowns, or magic infrastructure.

Settlements

Progression Anchors

Villages

Attract low-level adventurers and provide basic services.

Towns

Attract stronger adventurers and offer better gear, potions, training, and rumors.

Cities

Attract top-level characters and unlock resurrection, magic guilds, high-tier crafting, and large-scale quests.

Potential Services

  • Tavern: manually recruits adventurers for gold.
  • Inn: restores health over time.
  • Shop: sells potions and basic supplies.
  • Blacksmith: sells and repairs equipment.
  • Trainer: converts experience into levels or skill improvements.
  • Temple: healing, curse removal, resurrection.
  • Guild hall: party formation, contracts, bounties, policy controls.
  • Market: loot sale prices, resource flow, settlement economy.

Adventurers

Autonomous Agents

Adventurers are autonomous agents with persistent state. Early implementation should start with one generic archetype before class complexity is added.

Core Stats

  • Name and class.
  • Level and experience.
  • Health and max health.
  • Attack, defense, speed, and perception.
  • Gold, inventory, equipment, and potion count.
  • Current task and target.
  • Risk tolerance and personality traits.

Initial Classes

  • Fighter: durable melee adventurer, good starter class.
  • Rogue: faster travel, better loot chance, weaker in direct combat.
  • Cleric: healing and eventual resurrection support.
  • Mage: high damage, fragile, expensive scaling.

Parties

Risk Sharing

Adventurers can create, join, and leave parties. A party is a temporary or persistent group that travels, fights, and shares rewards.

Tradeoff

  • Solo adventuring is high risk and high reward.
  • Party adventuring is lower risk but rewards are split between members.
  • Parties can defeat stronger monsters and survive deeper expeditions.
  • Solo adventurers progress faster if they survive.

Behavior

  • Seek a party when a target is too dangerous alone.
  • Leave a party if rewards are too low, personalities clash, health is poor, or goals diverge.
  • Choose targets based on combined strength, supplies, travel distance, and expected reward.
  • Split gold, loot, and experience according to simple rules at first.

Initial Reward Model

  • Gold is split evenly.
  • Experience is split evenly or weighted by participation.
  • Items are assigned randomly, by class usefulness, or sold and split later.

Later Systems

  • Roles such as tank, healer, scout, damage dealer, and support.
  • Party reputation and named parties.
  • Party tactics, formations, morale, and leadership.
  • Contracts requiring minimum party strength.
  • Rescue parties for dead or stranded adventurers.

Adventurer Decision Model

Finite State Machine First

Adventurers should feel self-directed. Early behavior can use a simple finite state machine, then later evolve into utility scoring based on reward, risk, travel distance, supplies, and personality.

  • IdleInTownChoose whether to rest, shop, level up, or depart.
  • ChooseTargetSelect a nearby monster or location appropriate to level.
  • FindPartySeek allies when a target is too risky alone.
  • JoinPartyCoordinate with other adventurers and follow party decisions.
  • TravelToTargetMove through the world.
  • FightResolve combat against a monster.
  • LootCollect rewards.
  • ReturnToTownRetreat with loot or when health and supplies are low.
  • RecoverInTownHeal and resupply.
  • PossessedTemporarily controlled by the player until the spell expires.
  • DeadRemain dead until resurrection or permanent removal.

Monsters

Threats And Rewards

Monsters are world threats and resource generators. Early monsters should be simple static targets near the starting town, with later systems adding wandering monsters, lairs, patrols, boss monsters, ecology pressure, and attacks on settlements.

  • Type.
  • Level.
  • Health.
  • Attack and defense.
  • Aggro radius.
  • Loot table.
  • Experience reward.
  • Spawn location or lair.

World Locations

Goals And Pressure

Wilderness

Low-risk monster hunting.

Ruins

Higher loot, higher danger, limited visibility.

Dungeons

Multi-room expeditions with escalating risk.

Roads

Safer travel with possible ambushes.

Resource Nodes

Herbs, ore, relics, and monster dens.

Risk Signals

Communicate danger through distance, monster level, biome, rumors, or scouting.

World Direction

The world should start procedural because it is simpler to test, iterate, and rebalance while the game loop is still changing. For the first slice, no real world map is required: only a town, adventurers, and monsters.

Combat

Probability Rolls

Combat should be random, but the randomness should come from explicit probability calculations. Attacks, defenses, spells, resists, dodges, blocks, critical hits, and similar actions produce a fixed probability under the hood, then roll against it.

Formula Shape

hit_chance = f(attacker_accuracy, defender_evasion, level_delta, status_effects)

attack_hits = random(0..1) <= hit_chance

attack_crits = attack_hits && random(0..1) <= clamp(0.10 + attacker_crit_chance, 0.0, 1.0)

  1. Adventurer and monster enter combat.
  2. Combatants exchange attacks at fixed intervals.
  3. Each attack computes a success probability and rolls against it.
  4. Successful attacks roll separately for critical hits.
  5. Critical hits multiply attack, ability, or spell damage before defense by the attacker's crit damage.
  6. Successful attacks apply damage, mitigation, effects, or spell outcomes.
  7. Health reaches zero.
  8. Winner receives rewards or enters a post-combat state.

Later combat can add abilities, status effects, formations, party tactics, fleeing, potions, and class-specific logic.

Loot And Economy

Adventure To Settlement

Basic Loot Types

  • Gold.
  • Equipment.
  • Potions and consumables.
  • Materials.
  • Rare relics.

Early Loop

  • Monsters drop gold and experience.
  • Adventurers carry gold back to town.
  • Adventurers spend gold on healing or potions.
  • Excess wealth can indirectly benefit the settlement later.

The economy should eventually support visible flows: adventurers buy from shops, shops need stock, settlement upgrades improve available goods, and high-level adventurers require high-level services.

Death And Resurrection

Risk With Weight

Initial Behavior

  • A dead adventurer stops acting.
  • Death is logged visibly.
  • Their carried loot may be lost or dropped.

Long-Term Behavior

  • Resurrection is expensive.
  • Resurrection requires a high-level cleric, temple, rare item, or city-tier service.
  • Some deaths may be permanent depending on game settings or difficulty.

First Playable Slice Target

Small Complete Loop

The first slice should prove the smallest complete version of the loop.

  • A town exists as a home point.
  • One adventurer starts in the town.
  • One monster spawn exists outside the town.
  • The adventurer autonomously walks to a monster.
  • Combat resolves with probability-based attack rolls.
  • The adventurer gains gold and experience if successful.
  • The adventurer returns to the town.
  • The state is visible through logs, labels, or TestBridge events.

Not In This Slice

This does not need a world map, shops, classes, dungeons, parties, equipment, or full settlement progression yet.

Current Design Decisions

Locked For Now
  • Adventurers are hired manually with gold at first.
  • Auto-hiring may be added later.
  • If the player runs out of money, a desperate or downtrodden adventurer fallback may be considered.
  • Time is hybrid: tick-based mechanics with continuous movement and world time.
  • There is no pause planned for now.
  • Combat is random and should use explicit probability formulas followed by rolls.
  • Far-away adventurer simulation is undecided and irrelevant for the first slice.
  • The player is abstract and mostly idle for now.
  • Later player progression may include building additional structures or researching mechanics.
  • The world should start procedural for easier testing.
  • The first slice does not need a world map, only a town, adventurers, and monsters.

Open Design Questions

Decide Later
  • Are adventurers permanently simulated off-screen, or only near loaded areas?
  • What exact formula family should attack, defense, casting, dodge, block, and resist rolls use?
  • How generous should the no-money fallback adventurer option be?
